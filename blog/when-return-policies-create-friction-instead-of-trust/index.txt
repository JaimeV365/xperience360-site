a:T2401,<p class="mb-6">One of the most overlooked retention risks in retail right now has little to do with pricing or product quality. It often comes down to the gap between what a returns policy says, how clearly that policy is communicated at the point of purchase, and what actually happens when a customer tries to use it.</p><p class="mt-6 mb-6">Most restrictions on opened products exist for understandable operational reasons. However, when the wording is vague, the limitations are not made clear during the buying journey, and frontline staff apply the rule inconsistently, the policy stops protecting the business and starts eroding trust. A recent experience I had illustrates the problem rather neatly.</p>
<h3 id="the-experience"><strong>The Experience</strong></h3>
<p class="mb-6">I bought a tech product online: a speaker for my living room. When it arrived, I carefully unpacked it and tested it. However, the Bluetooth connection was not recognised by my other home devices. I put everything back exactly as it had been, apart from the security stickers. I could not undo those.</p><p class="mt-6 mb-6">I then went to the store to return it.</p>
<blockquote>
<ul>
<li>
<p class="mb-6">"Hi, I'm here to return this speaker that I bought online."</p>
</li>
<li>
<p class="mb-6">"This box has been opened and we can't accept a return."</p>
</li>
</ul>
</blockquote>
<p class="mb-6">That was the initial response. No inspection, no questions, no attempt to understand the situation. It was only when I explained that the product appeared to be faulty that the conversation changed. The return was then accepted and a full refund was processed without any real difficulty.</p><p class="mt-6 mb-6">To me, that was the first warning sign in this case. From a CX professional perspective, though, the broader issue is this: if a return is only accepted once a customer pushes back with the right phrase, the policy is not functioning as a fair policy. It becomes more like a filter for people willing to argue.</p>
<h3 id="why-the-policy-does-not-hold-up"><strong>Why the Policy Does Not Hold Up</strong></h3>
<p class="mb-6">Even leaving aside the fact that the product was not inspected before the initial refusal, the policy logic itself is, in my view, difficult to defend from a customer perspective. People need to open products to understand how they work, how they feel, whether they are compatible with their setup, and whether they meet reasonable expectations. That is not misuse. It is basic evaluation.</p>
<p class="mb-6">As I argued in <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="/blog/the-absurdity-of-closed-box-only-return-policies-a-customer-experience-fail">The Absurdity of Closed-Box-Only Return Policies: A Customer Experience Fail</a>, the core problem with these policies is that they often appear to protect the box more than the person deciding whether to buy what is inside it.</p>
<h3 id="buy-online-and-absorb-the-risk-yourself"><strong>Buy Online and Absorb the Risk Yourself</strong></h3>
<p class="mb-6">Many promotions are only available online. That matters, because online shopping removes the customer's chance to see, touch or compare the product properly before purchase. If the practical effect of the returns policy is that opening the product creates friction or uncertainty, then, from a CX perspective, the commercial risk can shift quietly from the retailer to the customer.</p><p class="mt-6 mb-6">That kind of restriction should not come as a nasty surprise at the returns counter. If important conditions apply to opened products, they should be made clear during checkout in the same way that price, delivery dates or warranty conditions are made clear. Customers should know the real conditions of the purchase before they pay, not afterwards.</p>
<h3 id="the-real-cost-of-a-bad-refusal"><strong>The Real Cost of a Bad Refusal</strong></h3>
<p class="mb-6">As a CX professional, what bothers me most is not the product failure itself. Products can fail. Expectations are sometimes not met. What matters is how the business responds when that happens.</p><p class="mt-6 mb-6">If the speaker had worked correctly, I would probably have been perfectly happy with the overall experience. Even when something goes wrong, customers can still become loyal advocates if they are treated fairly, respectfully and with a little common sense. In this case, it was not the faulty product that nearly damaged the relationship. It was the refusal to even look at it.</p>
<h3 id="what-happened-next"><strong>What Happened Next</strong></h3>
<p class="mb-6">After the store visit, I received a satisfaction survey. I gave it a poor rating and explained what had happened. That led to a follow-up exchange by email, and on 16 June 2026 I received this written response:</p>
<blockquote>
<p class="mb-6">"With online purchases, we allow customers to open their items and will still accept a return as long as it hasn't been used and is still within the returns period."</p>
</blockquote>
<p class="mb-6">That single sentence raises an important question. Either this was a one-off problem in which store staff were unclear about how the policy applied to online purchases, or the wording of the rule itself leaves too much room for interpretation. It allows opened items, but not items that have been "used". So where exactly is the line? How can a customer open and assess a product without doing anything that might later be interpreted as use?</p><p class="mt-6 mb-6">In my view, either explanation points to a CX weakness: unclear policy design, inconsistent application, or both. The customer experiences the same outcome either way - uncertainty, friction, and the feeling that fairness depends more on who serves them than on the policy itself.</p>
<h3 id="the-culture-of-complaint-and-why-fair-beats-friction"><strong>The Culture of Complaint, and Why Fair Beats Friction</strong></h3>
<p class="mb-6">From my CX professional perspective, there is a wider pattern here worth discussing. In many industries, customers who complain the loudest often end up receiving the most attention, the most escalation, and sometimes the best outcome. In effect, complaining can become the route to a more reasonable solution.</p><p class="mt-6 mb-6">That is not a sign of good customer experience design. If policies are clear, fair and consistently applied from the start, fewer customers need to escalate at all. That reduces complaint-handling effort, avoids unnecessary conflict, and creates better outcomes for both sides.</p>
<h3 id="happy-customers-should-not-need-to-argue"><strong>Happy Customers Should Not Need to Argue</strong></h3>
<p class="mb-6">From a customer experience point of view, this is the opposite of how a returns policy should work. In a better system, customers would not need to discover hidden distinctions, use the right wording, or push for an exception before someone considers a reasonable outcome.</p><p class="mt-6 mb-6">In practice, that might mean removing overly rigid restrictions on opened products, defining sensible exceptions for hygiene-sensitive or irreversible categories, and making it easier for staff to distinguish between genuine evaluation and meaningful product use.</p>
<p class="mb-6">The instinctive objection, of course, is margin pressure. Opened items can be harder to resell, and returns do create cost. However, that is only one side of the equation. The other side includes complaint-handling costs, damaged trust, reduced repeat purchase, negative word of mouth, and customers who simply choose to buy elsewhere next time.</p><p class="mt-6 mb-6">Weighed against all of that, a simpler and fairer policy may be less expensive than it first appears.</p>
<h3 id="the-amazon-comparison"><strong>The Amazon Comparison</strong></h3>
<p class="mb-6">Are you still wondering why Amazon continues to take such a large share of many retail categories? Its relatively low-friction returns experience may well be part of the answer. That does not mean every retailer should copy Amazon blindly. It does mean that the ease or difficulty of a return becomes part of how customers assess the risk of buying from you in the first place.</p><p class="mt-6 mb-6">If customers believe they will face friction, ambiguity or argument when something goes wrong, they may decide not to take the risk at all.</p>
<h3 id="the-takeaway"><strong>The Takeaway</strong></h3>
<p class="mb-6">From my CX professional perspective, if your returns policy depends on customers knowing what to say, or on escalating the issue before anyone properly considers the case, it is probably already costing you more than the returns it prevents.</p><p class="mt-6 mb-6">The answer is not necessarily to accept every opened box. It is to write the rule clearly, communicate it properly before purchase, and make sure frontline colleagues can apply it consistently and fairly.</p>
<p class="mb-6">A useful exercise for anyone reviewing a returns policy is to read the wording as if you were the customer standing at the counter with a peeled sticker and a perfectly reasonable question. If the answer is still not clear, the policy is not clear enough.</p>

<p class="mb-6">The instinctive objection, of course, is margin pressure. Opened items can be harder to resell, and returns do create cost. However, that is only one side of the equation. The other side includes complaint-handling costs, damaged trust, reduced repeat purchase, negative word of mouth, and customers who simply choose to buy elsewhere next time.</p><p class="mt-6 mb-6">Weighed against all of that, a simpler and fairer policy may be less expensive than it first appears.</p>
<h3 id="the-amazon-comparison"><strong>The Amazon Comparison</strong></h3>
<p class="mb-6">Are you still wondering why Amazon continues to take such a large share of many retail categories? Its relatively low-friction returns experience may well be part of the answer. That does not mean every retailer should copy Amazon blindly. It does mean that the ease or difficulty of a return becomes part of how customers assess the risk of buying from you in the first place.</p><p class="mt-6 mb-6">If customers believe they will face friction, ambiguity or argument when something goes wrong, they may decide not to take the risk at all.</p>
<h3 id="the-takeaway"><strong>The Takeaway</strong></h3>
<p class="mb-6">From my CX professional perspective, if your returns policy depends on customers knowing what to say, or on escalating the issue before anyone properly considers the case, it is probably already costing you more than the returns it prevents.</p><p class="mt-6 mb-6">The answer is not necessarily to accept every opened box. It is to write the rule clearly, communicate it properly before purchase, and make sure frontline colleagues can apply it consistently and fairly.</p>
<p class="mb-6">A useful exercise for anyone reviewing a returns policy is to read the wording as if you were the customer standing at the counter with a peeled sticker and a perfectly reasonable question. If the answer is still not clear, the policy is not clear enough.</p>
