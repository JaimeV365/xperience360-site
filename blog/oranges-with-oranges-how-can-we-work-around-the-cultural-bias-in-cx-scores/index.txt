9:T545,{"@context":"https://schema.org","@type":"BlogPosting","headline":"Oranges with Oranges: How Can We Work Around the Cultural Bias in CX Scores","description":"A CSAT of 4/5 or a high NPS® does not mean the same thing in Sydney and Bogotá. Drawing on Ipsos research into cultural response bias, this article explores why raw cross-country CX scores mislead — and what to do instead.","datePublished":"2026-10-19","dateModified":"2026-10-19","author":{"@type":"Person","name":"Jaime Valle","url":"https://www.linkedin.com/in/jaimevuk/"},"publisher":{"@type":"Organization","name":"Xperience 360","logo":{"@type":"ImageObject","url":"https://xperience-360.com/logo.png"}},"mainEntityOfPage":{"@type":"WebPage","@id":"https://xperience-360.com/blog/oranges-with-oranges-how-can-we-work-around-the-cultural-bias-in-cx-scores"},"keywords":"cultural bias, response bias, CX scores, NPS, Net Promoter Score, customer experience, CX, CX measurement, benchmarking, global CX, survey design, survey bias, bias in surveys, customer satisfaction, customer insights, customer loyalty, Apostles Model, customer behavior, customer segmentation, retention strategies, customer metrics, loyalty models, customer advocacy, loyalty marketing, brand strategy, Passives, customer feedback, surveys, feedback collection, cross-cultural, Ipsos","articleSection":"Blog"}b:T39fb,<p class="mb-6">I have been having some fascinating conversations lately about how to create value and add impact through a customer experience (CX) programme. The best practices all sound great when you dig into them. It pays to <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="/blog/think-before-surveying-customers">look at the data you already have before surveying customers</a>. It's also important to bring your internal teams' feedback into the equation, and to treat <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="/blog/the-critical-role-of-a-complaints-process-in-enhancing-customer-experience">complaints data</a> as a primary listening channel rather than an afterthought.</p>
<p class="mb-6">It's also important to be clever enough to add the <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="/blog/revisiting-the-apostles-model-understanding-customer-behaviour-in-todays-context">Apostles Model</a> lens and take a second look at all the wasted Passives hiding in your survey responses.</p><p class="mt-6 mb-6">However, at some point, you should consider the cultural element and context. A CSAT score of 4 out of 5 does not mean the same thing if it comes from a final customer or a reseller, or if it comes from Sydney, Australia, or Bogotá, Colombia. Not to mention when the survey was taken.</p>
<p class="mb-6">So, the recurring question is this: can we adapt our benchmarks and add a contextual element to adjust satisfaction and loyalty scales so that we can finally make meaningful comparisons?</p><p class="mt-6 mb-6">Ipsos might have found the answer.</p>
<h3 id="the-study"><strong>The Study</strong></h3>
<p class="mb-6">The paper is called <em><a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="https://www.ipsos.com/sites/default/files/ct/news/documents/2020-09/culturalbias2020ipsos.pdf">When Difference Doesn't Mean Different: Understanding Cultural Bias in Global CX Programmes</a></em> and was written by Fiona Moss and Bharath Vijayendra. First published in 2018, it was updated in August 2020 to include fresh data, such as responses collected after the start of the pandemic.</p>
<p class="mb-6">The starting point of the paper will be familiar to anyone who has run a multi-market programme. Global programmes offer better value for money, a degree of standardisation and the ability to identify global trends. However, comparing scores across countries at face value can be seriously misleading, whatever the sector or company — a theme that also shows up when <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="/blog/nps-score-of-95-too-good-to-be-true">raw NPS® scores look too good to be true</a>.</p>
<h3 id="three-ways-of-using-a-scale-without-meaning-to"><strong>Three Ways of Using a Scale (Without Meaning To)</strong></h3>
<p class="mb-6">The paper builds on the well-known concept of cultural response bias. This is the tendency of a country to consistently use certain points on a scale, regardless of the actual question asked. The three most commonly cited styles are:</p>
<ul>
<li>
<p class="mb-6"><strong>Acquiescence (ARS)</strong>: the tendency to agree to any question. This is seen frequently in Latin America, the Middle East, and some African markets.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p class="mb-6"><strong>Extreme (ERS)</strong>: the tendency to use the ends of the scale. This is typical of Latin America, mostly at the positive end (negative extremes are rare), while Asian markets are the least likely to choose extremes.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p class="mb-6"><strong>Middle (MRS)</strong>: the tendency to stay in the middle of the scale. This is more common in Asian markets and less so in Latin America.</p>
</li>
</ul>
<p class="mb-6">In other words, some respondents give out 10s as freely as free samples at the supermarket, while others treat a 9 as a family heirloom. That is a different kind of distortion from <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="/blog/the-hidden-bias-in-nps-surveys-why-colour-coding-can-distort-customer-feedback">colour-coding bias in NPS surveys</a> or <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="/blog/feedback-bias-the-complexities-of-honest-insights">relationship-driven feedback bias</a>, but it belongs in the same family of measurement traps.</p>
<h3 id="same-service-different-number"><strong>Same Service, Different Number?</strong></h3>
<p class="mb-6">To test this theory, Ipsos examined normative data from 2016 relating to the automotive, supermarket and retail banking sectors. In all three sectors, the proportion of customers scoring 8, 9 or 10 in Japan was dramatically lower than in Puerto Rico. Asian markets typically gave lower NPS scores, while Latin America and the US gave higher ones.</p><p class="mt-6 mb-6">For the 2020 update, they then built a benchmarking database from B2C CX studies called CX Lens, covering over 100 countries and more than 30 sectors. The pattern held:</p>
<ul>
<li>Latin American markets tend to score higher, while many Asian markets tend to score lower.</li>
<li>Markets in the Middle East tend to score higher.</li>
<li>In Africa, Anglophone markets tend to score higher than Francophone ones.</li>
<li>Even a question about how optimistic people feel about the next 12 months followed the same pattern. Therefore, this is not only about how people rate brands.</li>
</ul>
<p class="mb-6">Before celebrating your Bogotá team's NPS score or putting your Tokyo team on a performance plan, consider whether you are measuring service or simply how people use a scale.</p>
<h3 id="but-beware-of-sweeping-statements"><strong>But Beware of Sweeping Statements</strong></h3>
<p class="mb-6">To their credit, the authors acknowledge that it is not as simple as "Asia low, Latin America high".</p>
<ul>
<li>There are exceptions: for example, Cambodia, the Philippines, Indonesia and Vietnam all give high scores, in contrast with other Asian markets.</li>
<li>Expectations can also get in the way. Market maturity and competitiveness shape customer expectations, and the paper admits that it is difficult to distinguish between expectations and response style.</li>
<li>The metric matters. Its effect is less visible in a mean score than in a top-box metric — another reminder that <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="/blog/the-confusion-between-nps-and-satisfaction-a-missed-opportunity-in-cx-measurement">NPS and satisfaction are not interchangeable</a>.</li>
</ul>
<p class="mb-6">Clearly, something cultural is going on, but it is not a rule that can be applied with a calculator.</p>
<h3 id="the-fixes-on-the-menu"><strong>The Fixes on the Menu</strong></h3>
<p class="mb-6">Ipsos reviews three techniques that make cross-country comparisons more feasible. Each technique has its own trade-offs:</p>
<ul>
<li>
<p class="mb-6"><strong>Standardisation or normalisation</strong> involves adjusting scores against the average for each individual or group. This method allows you to aggregate results and provides a relative view, but it can eliminate genuine differences between countries and requires a large number of attributes.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p class="mb-6"><strong>Studying trends over time</strong> involves looking at each market against itself. This is a reliable way to monitor progress, but it means that countries remain in their silos and comparing markets remains unreliable.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p class="mb-6"><strong>Calibration based on expectation questions</strong> involves asking whether the experience was better, the same or worse than expected, and using this information to adjust the scale. This method enables cross-market comparison, but it results in two sets of numbers being used, requires statistical adjustment factors and assumes that expectation levels are consistent between markets.</p>
</li>
</ul>
<h3 id="what-ipsos-recommends"><strong>What Ipsos Recommends</strong></h3>
<p class="mb-6">The route they take depends on whether or not you collect competitor information.</p>
<h4 id="1-no-competitor-data-use-reference-brands"><strong>1. No competitor data? Use reference brands</strong></h4>
<p class="mb-6">Add a question about large, well-known global brands that deliver a similar service in every market. Anyone's score for these brands reflects three factors: how the brand performs, the respondent's cultural response bias and their socio-demographic profile. If the brand's performance changes very little between markets and you know the demographics, you can isolate the cultural bias and calculate a calibration factor to apply to your own KPIs.</p><p class="mt-6 mb-6">The downside is that the questionnaire will be longer and there will be two versions of your figures (original and recalibrated). The advantage is that these factors can be applied to any KPI and only need to be recalculated periodically.</p>
<h4 id="2-competitor-data-rank-dont-score"><strong>2. Competitor data? Rank, don't score</strong></h4>
<p class="mb-6">Instead of looking at your score in isolation, consider how respondents rate you against other brands in your sector. This could be the percentage of respondents who rate you the highest or a ranking-based metric such as Ipsos' Attitudinal Equity.</p><p class="mt-6 mb-6">The logic is simple: rank matters. A score of 9 out of 10 is only good if it beats your competitors'. Ipsos illustrates this with two customers who both give you a 9: for one of them, you are clearly in first place. For the other, you are joint last because your competitors got a 9 and a 10. Same number, completely different story.</p>
<p class="mb-6">Ipsos also reports that attitudinal equity correlates much better with real market share (0.85) than satisfaction (0.57) or purchase intent (0.30). However, it is their own metric, validated on their own database, so a healthy dose of scepticism is in order, as with any vendor-published study. However, the principle of interpreting a score in context is difficult to dispute.</p>
<h3 id="my-take-what-i-would-do-with-this"><strong>My Take: What I Would Do With This</strong></h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Design before you analyse.</strong> Use the same scales everywhere, treat "don't know" and "not applicable" consistently, and ensure that translations convey the same meaning.</li>
<li><strong>Focus on trends before benchmarks.</strong> First, compare each market with itself. Your own history is the one comparison free from cultural bias.</li>
<li><strong>Think twice before ranking country teams by raw NPS.</strong> Especially if bonuses are attached to it. You may end up rewarding response style rather than service.</li>
<li><strong>Rank wherever you can.</strong> If you can collect competitor information, a score relative to the market tells you far more than an absolute number does.</li>
</ul>
<p class="mb-6">These design choices sit naturally inside a broader <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="/blog/how-to-implement-a-customer-experience-programme-that-actually-works">CX programme that actually works</a>: measurement without context is just noise with a dashboard.</p>
<h3 id="what-the-paper-doesnt-answer-yet"><strong>What the Paper Doesn't Answer (Yet)</strong></h3>
<p class="mb-6">My original question remains open here:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>There is no conversion formula.</strong> The paper never specifies what 80% in Japan equates to in Argentina, and I have not found an accessible study that does so. Ipsos's own data suggests why such a formula would be unreliable: the pattern changes depending on the sector and metric; it is stronger for the top box than the mean; there are exceptions within every region; and expectations are intertwined with response style. Any factor would only apply to one sector, one metric and one scale. The reference-brand method produces such factors, but the paper does not publish any.</li>
<li><strong>B2B and personas.</strong> The figures presented come from B2C studies. As the paper does not show a B2B versus B2C split, the question of reseller versus final customer remains.</li>
<li><strong>The moment of truth.</strong> There is no information here on how much the timing and type of survey (relational or transactional) change the picture.</li>
<li><strong>Within-country differences.</strong> Ipsos itself warns against continent-wide generalisations, and one country does not represent an entire culture. A customer in Sydney is not representative of all customers in Australia.</li>
<li><strong>What happens to Passives?</strong> Here is an idea that I have not tested: if people in some markets avoid the extremes, are 7s and 8s closer to promoters than the standard NPS rules suggest? This is precisely the area in which an <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="/blog/revisiting-the-apostles-model-understanding-customer-behaviour-in-todays-context">Apostles Model</a> lens could be invaluable.</li>
</ul>
<h3 id="conclusion-no-magic-formula-so-build-your-own"><strong>Conclusion: No Magic Formula, So Build Your Own</strong></h3>
<p class="mb-6">Cultural response bias is real, systematic and measurable, and the Ipsos paper provides a useful overview of it. However, it does not provide a conversion table, and I would be wary of anyone who offers one without specifying the sector, metric and scale on which it is based. The honest conclusion is that there is no formula that can be borrowed. If you want comparable numbers across markets, you have to build the calibration into your own programme.</p>
<ol>
<li>Design for the bias from day one. Use the same scales, consistent options and faithful translations.</li>
<li>Read every market against its own trend before comparing it with any other market.</li>
<li>Collect the context that enables you to make adjustments. Ask about competitor ratings if you can, or use a reference-brand question if you cannot, so you can rank instead of score.</li>
</ol>
<p class="mb-6">If you have come across any research on B2B versus B2C response patterns, persona-level benchmarks or the effect of the timing of the survey on the score, I would love to hear about it.</p>

[&_a.download-link]:before:text-lg [&_a.download-link]:before:leading-none [&_a.download-link]:before:font-normale:T39fb,<p class="mb-6">I have been having some fascinating conversations lately about how to create value and add impact through a customer experience (CX) programme. The best practices all sound great when you dig into them. It pays to <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="/blog/think-before-surveying-customers">look at the data you already have before surveying customers</a>. It's also important to bring your internal teams' feedback into the equation, and to treat <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="/blog/the-critical-role-of-a-complaints-process-in-enhancing-customer-experience">complaints data</a> as a primary listening channel rather than an afterthought.</p>
<p class="mb-6">It's also important to be clever enough to add the <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="/blog/revisiting-the-apostles-model-understanding-customer-behaviour-in-todays-context">Apostles Model</a> lens and take a second look at all the wasted Passives hiding in your survey responses.</p><p class="mt-6 mb-6">However, at some point, you should consider the cultural element and context. A CSAT score of 4 out of 5 does not mean the same thing if it comes from a final customer or a reseller, or if it comes from Sydney, Australia, or Bogotá, Colombia. Not to mention when the survey was taken.</p>
<p class="mb-6">So, the recurring question is this: can we adapt our benchmarks and add a contextual element to adjust satisfaction and loyalty scales so that we can finally make meaningful comparisons?</p><p class="mt-6 mb-6">Ipsos might have found the answer.</p>
<h3 id="the-study"><strong>The Study</strong></h3>
<p class="mb-6">The paper is called <em><a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="https://www.ipsos.com/sites/default/files/ct/news/documents/2020-09/culturalbias2020ipsos.pdf">When Difference Doesn't Mean Different: Understanding Cultural Bias in Global CX Programmes</a></em> and was written by Fiona Moss and Bharath Vijayendra. First published in 2018, it was updated in August 2020 to include fresh data, such as responses collected after the start of the pandemic.</p>
<p class="mb-6">The starting point of the paper will be familiar to anyone who has run a multi-market programme. Global programmes offer better value for money, a degree of standardisation and the ability to identify global trends. However, comparing scores across countries at face value can be seriously misleading, whatever the sector or company — a theme that also shows up when <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="/blog/nps-score-of-95-too-good-to-be-true">raw NPS® scores look too good to be true</a>.</p>
<h3 id="three-ways-of-using-a-scale-without-meaning-to"><strong>Three Ways of Using a Scale (Without Meaning To)</strong></h3>
<p class="mb-6">The paper builds on the well-known concept of cultural response bias. This is the tendency of a country to consistently use certain points on a scale, regardless of the actual question asked. The three most commonly cited styles are:</p>
<ul>
<li>
<p class="mb-6"><strong>Acquiescence (ARS)</strong>: the tendency to agree to any question. This is seen frequently in Latin America, the Middle East, and some African markets.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p class="mb-6"><strong>Extreme (ERS)</strong>: the tendency to use the ends of the scale. This is typical of Latin America, mostly at the positive end (negative extremes are rare), while Asian markets are the least likely to choose extremes.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p class="mb-6"><strong>Middle (MRS)</strong>: the tendency to stay in the middle of the scale. This is more common in Asian markets and less so in Latin America.</p>
</li>
</ul>
<p class="mb-6">In other words, some respondents give out 10s as freely as free samples at the supermarket, while others treat a 9 as a family heirloom. That is a different kind of distortion from <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="/blog/the-hidden-bias-in-nps-surveys-why-colour-coding-can-distort-customer-feedback">colour-coding bias in NPS surveys</a> or <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="/blog/feedback-bias-the-complexities-of-honest-insights">relationship-driven feedback bias</a>, but it belongs in the same family of measurement traps.</p>
<h3 id="same-service-different-number"><strong>Same Service, Different Number?</strong></h3>
<p class="mb-6">To test this theory, Ipsos examined normative data from 2016 relating to the automotive, supermarket and retail banking sectors. In all three sectors, the proportion of customers scoring 8, 9 or 10 in Japan was dramatically lower than in Puerto Rico. Asian markets typically gave lower NPS scores, while Latin America and the US gave higher ones.</p><p class="mt-6 mb-6">For the 2020 update, they then built a benchmarking database from B2C CX studies called CX Lens, covering over 100 countries and more than 30 sectors. The pattern held:</p>
<ul>
<li>Latin American markets tend to score higher, while many Asian markets tend to score lower.</li>
<li>Markets in the Middle East tend to score higher.</li>
<li>In Africa, Anglophone markets tend to score higher than Francophone ones.</li>
<li>Even a question about how optimistic people feel about the next 12 months followed the same pattern. Therefore, this is not only about how people rate brands.</li>
</ul>
<p class="mb-6">Before celebrating your Bogotá team's NPS score or putting your Tokyo team on a performance plan, consider whether you are measuring service or simply how people use a scale.</p>
<h3 id="but-beware-of-sweeping-statements"><strong>But Beware of Sweeping Statements</strong></h3>
<p class="mb-6">To their credit, the authors acknowledge that it is not as simple as "Asia low, Latin America high".</p>
<ul>
<li>There are exceptions: for example, Cambodia, the Philippines, Indonesia and Vietnam all give high scores, in contrast with other Asian markets.</li>
<li>Expectations can also get in the way. Market maturity and competitiveness shape customer expectations, and the paper admits that it is difficult to distinguish between expectations and response style.</li>
<li>The metric matters. Its effect is less visible in a mean score than in a top-box metric — another reminder that <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="/blog/the-confusion-between-nps-and-satisfaction-a-missed-opportunity-in-cx-measurement">NPS and satisfaction are not interchangeable</a>.</li>
</ul>
<p class="mb-6">Clearly, something cultural is going on, but it is not a rule that can be applied with a calculator.</p>
<h3 id="the-fixes-on-the-menu"><strong>The Fixes on the Menu</strong></h3>
<p class="mb-6">Ipsos reviews three techniques that make cross-country comparisons more feasible. Each technique has its own trade-offs:</p>
<ul>
<li>
<p class="mb-6"><strong>Standardisation or normalisation</strong> involves adjusting scores against the average for each individual or group. This method allows you to aggregate results and provides a relative view, but it can eliminate genuine differences between countries and requires a large number of attributes.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p class="mb-6"><strong>Studying trends over time</strong> involves looking at each market against itself. This is a reliable way to monitor progress, but it means that countries remain in their silos and comparing markets remains unreliable.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p class="mb-6"><strong>Calibration based on expectation questions</strong> involves asking whether the experience was better, the same or worse than expected, and using this information to adjust the scale. This method enables cross-market comparison, but it results in two sets of numbers being used, requires statistical adjustment factors and assumes that expectation levels are consistent between markets.</p>
</li>
</ul>
<h3 id="what-ipsos-recommends"><strong>What Ipsos Recommends</strong></h3>
<p class="mb-6">The route they take depends on whether or not you collect competitor information.</p>
<h4 id="1-no-competitor-data-use-reference-brands"><strong>1. No competitor data? Use reference brands</strong></h4>
<p class="mb-6">Add a question about large, well-known global brands that deliver a similar service in every market. Anyone's score for these brands reflects three factors: how the brand performs, the respondent's cultural response bias and their socio-demographic profile. If the brand's performance changes very little between markets and you know the demographics, you can isolate the cultural bias and calculate a calibration factor to apply to your own KPIs.</p><p class="mt-6 mb-6">The downside is that the questionnaire will be longer and there will be two versions of your figures (original and recalibrated). The advantage is that these factors can be applied to any KPI and only need to be recalculated periodically.</p>
<h4 id="2-competitor-data-rank-dont-score"><strong>2. Competitor data? Rank, don't score</strong></h4>
<p class="mb-6">Instead of looking at your score in isolation, consider how respondents rate you against other brands in your sector. This could be the percentage of respondents who rate you the highest or a ranking-based metric such as Ipsos' Attitudinal Equity.</p><p class="mt-6 mb-6">The logic is simple: rank matters. A score of 9 out of 10 is only good if it beats your competitors'. Ipsos illustrates this with two customers who both give you a 9: for one of them, you are clearly in first place. For the other, you are joint last because your competitors got a 9 and a 10. Same number, completely different story.</p>
<p class="mb-6">Ipsos also reports that attitudinal equity correlates much better with real market share (0.85) than satisfaction (0.57) or purchase intent (0.30). However, it is their own metric, validated on their own database, so a healthy dose of scepticism is in order, as with any vendor-published study. However, the principle of interpreting a score in context is difficult to dispute.</p>
<h3 id="my-take-what-i-would-do-with-this"><strong>My Take: What I Would Do With This</strong></h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Design before you analyse.</strong> Use the same scales everywhere, treat "don't know" and "not applicable" consistently, and ensure that translations convey the same meaning.</li>
<li><strong>Focus on trends before benchmarks.</strong> First, compare each market with itself. Your own history is the one comparison free from cultural bias.</li>
<li><strong>Think twice before ranking country teams by raw NPS.</strong> Especially if bonuses are attached to it. You may end up rewarding response style rather than service.</li>
<li><strong>Rank wherever you can.</strong> If you can collect competitor information, a score relative to the market tells you far more than an absolute number does.</li>
</ul>
<p class="mb-6">These design choices sit naturally inside a broader <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="/blog/how-to-implement-a-customer-experience-programme-that-actually-works">CX programme that actually works</a>: measurement without context is just noise with a dashboard.</p>
<h3 id="what-the-paper-doesnt-answer-yet"><strong>What the Paper Doesn't Answer (Yet)</strong></h3>
<p class="mb-6">My original question remains open here:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>There is no conversion formula.</strong> The paper never specifies what 80% in Japan equates to in Argentina, and I have not found an accessible study that does so. Ipsos's own data suggests why such a formula would be unreliable: the pattern changes depending on the sector and metric; it is stronger for the top box than the mean; there are exceptions within every region; and expectations are intertwined with response style. Any factor would only apply to one sector, one metric and one scale. The reference-brand method produces such factors, but the paper does not publish any.</li>
<li><strong>B2B and personas.</strong> The figures presented come from B2C studies. As the paper does not show a B2B versus B2C split, the question of reseller versus final customer remains.</li>
<li><strong>The moment of truth.</strong> There is no information here on how much the timing and type of survey (relational or transactional) change the picture.</li>
<li><strong>Within-country differences.</strong> Ipsos itself warns against continent-wide generalisations, and one country does not represent an entire culture. A customer in Sydney is not representative of all customers in Australia.</li>
<li><strong>What happens to Passives?</strong> Here is an idea that I have not tested: if people in some markets avoid the extremes, are 7s and 8s closer to promoters than the standard NPS rules suggest? This is precisely the area in which an <a target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" href="/blog/revisiting-the-apostles-model-understanding-customer-behaviour-in-todays-context">Apostles Model</a> lens could be invaluable.</li>
</ul>
<h3 id="conclusion-no-magic-formula-so-build-your-own"><strong>Conclusion: No Magic Formula, So Build Your Own</strong></h3>
<p class="mb-6">Cultural response bias is real, systematic and measurable, and the Ipsos paper provides a useful overview of it. However, it does not provide a conversion table, and I would be wary of anyone who offers one without specifying the sector, metric and scale on which it is based. The honest conclusion is that there is no formula that can be borrowed. If you want comparable numbers across markets, you have to build the calibration into your own programme.</p>
<ol>
<li>Design for the bias from day one. Use the same scales, consistent options and faithful translations.</li>
<li>Read every market against its own trend before comparing it with any other market.</li>
<li>Collect the context that enables you to make adjustments. Ask about competitor ratings if you can, or use a reference-brand question if you cannot, so you can rank instead of score.</li>
</ol>
<p class="mb-6">If you have come across any research on B2B versus B2C response patterns, persona-level benchmarks or the effect of the timing of the survey on the score, I would love to hear about it.</p>
